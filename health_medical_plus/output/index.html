A new drug treatment for head and neck cancer

On Nov. 5, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the use of a new drug to treat head and neck cancer, the first in seven years. Called amivantamab, the drug is the first to target two growth receptors for head and neck cancer simultaneously, reducing the cancer's ability to adapt to the treatment. The FDA granted amivantamab accelerated approval because of the promising results it showed during clinical trials, which were led by Yale School of Medicine researchers.

FDA approval offers a new endocrine therapy option for people with early-stage breast cancer

About 70% of breast cancers are hormone receptor-positive, meaning the cancer cells have receptors that allow hormones such as estrogen to promote their growth. For people with early-stage hormone receptor (HR)-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer, surgery is often followed by endocrine therapy to reduce the chance that the cancer will return. Depending on the patient, treatment can include tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, often taken for five years or longer.

Inhaled antibiotics may enhance immunotherapy by targeting bacteria in lung metastases

Researchers at the Leibniz Institute for Immunotherapy (LIT) in Regensburg, Germany, at the University of Milan and at the National Cancer Institute (INT) in Milan, Italy, have identified tumor-associated bacteria in lung metastases as a potential barrier to immunotherapy. In mice, aerosolized antibiotics disrupted this immunosuppressive environment and enhanced treatment responses, including complete tumor regressions. The study is published in Cancer Immunology Research.
